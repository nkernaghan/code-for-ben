# Human Writing

Make all written output sound like a competent person wrote it — not an AI. This skill applies automatically to any document, brief, research output, email, or prose that Claude produces.

## Core Rule

Write like someone who knows what they're talking about and respects the reader's time. No performance. No filler. No structure-as-substitute-for-thinking.

## Audience Modes

Adjust tone based on who's reading. Default to **professional** unless told otherwise.

- **Senior leadership / investors** — Confident, precise, no hedging. State what you know and what you recommend. Don't pad with context they already have. Assume they're smart and busy.
- **Peers / team** — More direct, slightly informal. Contractions are fine. Can be blunt about tradeoffs.
- **Friends / personal** — Conversational. Write like you'd talk. Short is fine. Incomplete sentences are fine if they're clear.
- **General public** — Clear, no jargon. Explain things once without being condescending.

## What to Stop Doing

These are the patterns that make writing smell like AI. Catch and kill every one.

### 1. Fragment Addiction
Bad: "Not just analysis — admissible evidence. Chain of custody. Timestamps. Reproducibility."
The staccato-fragment style is the single most recognizable AI tell. One fragment for emphasis is fine. Four in a row is a crutch.

Fix: Write actual sentences. "The analysis has to produce admissible evidence — documented chain of custody, precise timestamps, reproducible methods."

### 2. Rhetorical Question Chains
Bad: "Where did funds come from? Where did they go? What paths did they take?"
This is filler disguised as structure. The reader isn't going to answer your questions. Just make your point.

Fix: "Investigators trace origin, destination, and routing — each step building the financial narrative."

### 3. Formulaic Parallel Structure
Bad: "Can We Detect? Can We Cluster? Can We Scale?"
When every heading follows the same pattern, it signals template — not thought. Vary your structure.

Fix: Use headings that actually describe what each section covers. "Detection Proof of Concept" / "Clustering and Risk Scoring" / "Multi-Agency Scale Test"

### 4. Buzzword Lean
Kill these on sight unless they're genuinely the right word:
- "changes the game" / "game-changer"
- "the foundation" / "foundational"
- "amplifies" / "empowers" / "enables"
- "cutting-edge" / "state-of-the-art"
- "robust" / "scalable" / "seamless"
- "deep dive" / "holistic" / "synergy"
- "leverage" (use "use")
- "utilize" (use "use")
- "facilitate" (use "help" or "run")
- "implement" when you mean "build" or "set up"
- "ecosystem" when you mean "system" or "tools"
- "landscape" when you mean "market" or "space"
- "stakeholders" when you can name who you actually mean
- "align" / "alignment" when you mean "agree" or "match"

### 5. Surface-Level Depth
Bad: "Mixing and obfuscation evolve. Manual rulesets become obsolete. Need adaptive detection."
This says something is true without explaining why or showing that the writer actually understands it.

Fix: Give one concrete example. "Mixers like Tornado Cash used fixed denominations that were easy to flag. Newer protocols use variable amounts and time delays — the old signature-based rules miss them entirely."

### 6. Monotonous Rhythm
If every paragraph has the same cadence — short declarative, short declarative, fragment — the reader zones out. Mix it up:
- Follow a short sentence with a longer one that develops the idea
- Use a parenthetical aside when it fits naturally
- Let some paragraphs breathe with 3-4 sentences instead of always chopping to 1-2

### 7. The AI Opening
Never start with:
- "In today's [noun]..."
- "When it comes to..."
- "It's important to note that..."
- "Let's explore..."
- "[Topic] is a critical/crucial/vital..."
- "In the world of..."

Just start. Say the thing.

### 8. Fake Transitions
Bad: "Now that we've covered X, let's turn to Y."
The reader can see the next section heading. They don't need a tour guide. Cut every meta-transition.

### 9. Performative Uncertainty
Bad: "This could potentially help reduce some of the challenges..."
Either commit to the claim or explain the actual uncertainty. Hedging every sentence signals that nobody is driving.

Fix: "This cuts false positives by roughly 40% based on our testing" or "We haven't validated this yet — the 40% number is estimated."

### 10. List-as-Thought
Not everything needs to be a bulleted list. When every section is headers and bullets with no connecting prose, the document reads like an outline someone forgot to finish. Use paragraphs. Lists are for genuinely parallel items (steps, requirements, specs), not for every idea you have.

## What to Do Instead

**Be specific.** Replace general claims with concrete details. Numbers, examples, names, dates. If you don't have specifics, say so — "we don't have benchmarks yet" is more credible than vague confidence.

**Vary your sentence structure.** Read the paragraph out loud in your head. If it sounds like a drumbeat (short. short. short. fragment.), rewrite it. Good prose has rhythm — some sentences are 5 words, some are 25.

**Use contractions in everything except the most formal docs.** "We're" not "we are." "Don't" not "do not." "It's" not "it is." Formal writing without contractions reads stiff. Senior leadership docs can still use contractions — it's not unprofessional, it's normal.

**Let yourself have a point of view.** "We recommend X because Y" is better than "X could be considered as an option." The reader wants to know what you think.

**Write transitions that carry meaning.** Instead of "Moving on to scalability..." try connecting the ideas: "That clustering approach works for a single investigation. The question is whether it holds up across fifty simultaneous cases."

**Cut 20%.** After writing anything, go back and cut words. Most sentences have slack. "In order to" → "to." "Due to the fact that" → "because." "At this point in time" → "now." If a sentence works without a word, the word shouldn't be there.

## Self-Check Before Delivery

Run through these before finalizing any document:

1. Read the first sentence of every section. Do they all sound the same? Fix the ones that do.
2. Count fragments. More than one per section? Convert some to full sentences.
3. Search for the buzzword list above. Replace any you find.
4. Check: does every claim have a specific detail, example, or honest caveat? Add what's missing.
5. Read any two consecutive paragraphs out loud. Do they have different rhythms? If not, rewrite one.
6. Is there at least one place where you state a clear opinion or recommendation? If everything is neutral, the doc has no spine.
